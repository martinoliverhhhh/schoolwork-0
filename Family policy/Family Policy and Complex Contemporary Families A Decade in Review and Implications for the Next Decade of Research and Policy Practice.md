# Family Policy and Complex Contemporary Families: A Decade in Review and Implications for the Next Decade of Research and Policy Practice

Prior Journal of Marriage and Family decade-in-review articles have grappled with the definition and role of family policy for research and policy practice while emphasizing its value to both. In this article, we begin with a broad conceptualization of family policy that encompasses actions intended to achieve explicitly stated goals for families (explicit policies) and those that affect families without an explicitly stated goal for doing so (implicit policies), which we believe provides a solid framework for guiding and understanding both research and practice in the field. Second, we review major U.S. policy initiatives in the past decade and their documented and potential effects on families. Third, we describe several key aspects by which contemporary families have become more diverse and complex. Fourth, we discuss the implications of ongoing family complexity for public policies. We conclude with a discussion about future research and policy development in the context of contemporary family complexity. 

Families play a crucial role in sustaining human populations: Parents are charged with nurturing, socializing, and supporting dependent children until they can (hopefully) become independent and responsible citizens, workers, and commu nity members—potentially going on to have their own children, and the intergenerational cycle continues. Yet, not every family provides the same resources and opportunities, and the past decade has pointed to growing diversity and inequality in family experiences that are linked with inequality in a host of outcomes (Duncan & Murnane, 2016; Lundberg, Pollak, & Stearns, 2016). Public policies can affect the formation of families in the first place and the circumstances and conditions under which fam ilies exist, including (sometimes) providing or subsidizing alternative care when parents are not able to care for their children. 

Since 1990, “family policy” has been among the topics addressed in the Journal of Marriage and Family decade-in-review issue, suggesting the importance within family research of understanding how the public sphere interacts with, supports, and potentially undermines families as social institutions. The earlie reviews introduced the concept of family policy and highlighted important policy changes in the 1980s, 1990s and 2000s. We extend this prior work by providing a broader view of what we argue should be considered as “family policy,” and we summarize key policy changes—and changes in family patterns with policy implications—during the past decade, including policy changes and research publications from 2010 through 2019. 

We begin by outlining a conceptualization of family policy that we believe provides a useful framework for guiding and understanding both research and policy practice in the field. Second, we review major U.S. policy initiatives in the past decade and their documented and potential effects on families. Third, we describe several aspects by which contemporary family forms are increasingly complex. Fourth, we discuss the implications of ongoing family complexity for public policies that were largely crafted in an era when families were much less diverse and complex. We conclude with recommendations for future research and policy development in the context of contemporary family complexity. Throughout, we primarily focus on the United States. However, we document similarities and differences in both family and policy trends in the United States relative to other advanced industrialized nations. 

## Family Policy: A Conceptual Framework

Social policy is broadly equated with the functions of the welfare state, or governmental actions, including direct intervention as well as laws and regulations (for which effects may vary as a function of implementation), intended to influence the well-being of members of a society, often outside of what the market alone provides. Its key domains include income support, education, health care, employment, housing, and social services. Policy levers in these areas may include direct or indirect (e.g., tax) transfers in the form of cash or in-kind assistance as well as direct service provision. These interventions may be publicly or privately administered or provided but typically include some form of government intervention in the form of mandates, regulations, funding mechanisms, or incentives. Systems can be more generous with a social insurance model in which preventing, confronting, and addressing social problems is adopted as an integral and primary function of government, or less generous with a residual model, in which government’s role is limited to intervening only when traditional institutions such as the labor market, family, and community are unable to provide a minimum standard of living or quality of life. Regardless of the particular definition, social policy intrinsically implies value judg ments about the opportunities to which every member of society should have access and the potential redistribution of resources that migh be required (Titmuss, 1974). 

As noted in three prior Journal of Marriage and Family decade-in-review articles (Aldous & Dumon, 1990; Bogenschneider, 2000; Bogenschneider & Corbett, 2010), family policy represents a subfield or subdomain of social policy. In its broadest sense, family policy can be defined as 

“what government does to and for children and their families, in particular those public policies (laws, regulations) that are designed to affect the situation of families with children—or individuals in their family roles—and those that have clear consequences for such families even though the impacts may not have been intended.” (Kamerman, 1996, p. 31) 

Notably, this definition—similar to most definitions of family policy—is focused on families with children. This reflects that family policy is grounded in the principle that the family is the primary institution for childrearing and social ization and that societal investments in families are primarily to support the healthy development of the next generation of workers and citizens; it also implies that family policy includes all fam ilies with children rather than some subset (e.g., low-income families) thereof, and that invest ments in the family unit (including parents) are necessary to support children’s development into productive citizens (Kamerman, 1996). 

Although we recognize that families come in a variety of forms that may or may not involve legal, biological, or intergenerational ties (e.g., unmarried couples without children), here we retain the focus on families with children. To provide some sense of scope, we note that, based on the 2018 Current Population Survey, there were 127.6 million households in the United States in 2018, of which 83.1 million were classified as family households (members related by birth, marriage, or adoption). Of these, 34.5 million (or 41%) included their “own” 

children younger than age 18, and these family households included 68.0 million children (or about 2.0 children per family; U.S. Census Bureau, 2018). These represent point-in-time estimates, and the numbers and characteristics of families certainly change over time. 

While retaining the focus on families with children, we argue that the scope of family policy should be broadened across two key domains. First, policies under consideration should not be limited to those explicitly targeted toward families with children, but should also include those without a family focus that implicitly influence families. Second, policies should be assessed in recognition of growing family complexity and diversity, where the implications—especially with respect to broader patterns of inequality—may be quite different than during earlier times which were characterized by (somewhat) greater stability and homogeneity in family forms. Indeed, Seltzer (2019) argues that growing family complexity and diversity has increased uncertainty about family stability and relationships and thereby weakened the private safety net provided by families for their members. Moreover, family complexity and related uncertainty are particularly salient among socially and economically disadvantaged populations and are also linked to ongoing, including intergenerational, transmission of social and economic disadvantage and inequality. As such, we argue that reforming family policies to better serve the needs of diverse and complex contemporary families is crucial for promoting the current and future well-being of the U.S. population. 

## Explicit and Implicit Policies

Drawing on the work of Kamerman and Kahn (1976, 1978, 1989, 2001), we advocate a broad conceptualization of family policy that encompasses actions intended to achieve explicitly stated goals for families (explicit policies) as well as those that affect families without an explicitly stated goal for doing so (implicit polices); the latter includes potential consequences that may be intended or unintended (Kamerman & Kahn, 1976). Family policies may have goals or consequences in areas such as fertility, cohabitation, marriage, divorce, gender equity, labor market participation, work–life balance, economic resources, and the cost of childrearing. Explicit policies, for example, include tax and transfer programs that are con ditioned on having or caring for children as well as family planning, family leave, family law, child welfare (child abuse and neglect), child care, maternal and child health interventions, and the like. Implicit policies may not be directly targeted at families or depend on having a child, but may affect families nonetheless; elements of income tax policy, housing policies, and health care policies, for example, may not be directed at families, but may differentially affect single individuals, or partnered individuals who do not have dependent children, versus those in two-generation family units. 

We note that this is a somewhat broader perspective than that articulated by Bogenschneider (2000, p. 1137) and Bogenschneider and Corbett (2010) in the last two Journal of Mar riage and Family decade-in-review articles on this topic; they advocated more narrowly defin ing family policy to include only explicit poli cies targeted at families and reserving a separate framing, the “family perspective on policy making,” for policies with implicit consequences for families. We prefer the more inclusive approach for two primary reasons. First, we find the family policy versus family perspective on policy mak ing distinction to be somewhat blurred. Take, for example, housing assistance policy: In general, rental housing subsidies (unit- and tenant-based assistance in the United States, such as pub lic housing and Section 8 vouchers) would be excluded from family policy but included in the family perspective on policy making. However, such policies give preference to families with children and consider the number (and gender) of children in making benefit deter minations (e.g., determining size of unit and number of bedrooms). As such, we argue that this is de facto family policy and believe it is both unnecessary and overly complicating to categorize it otherwise. Second, we argue that a more inclusive approach is particularly useful for making cross-national comparisons in a context in which most other advanced indus trialized countries have adopted a more consistent and holistic approach to family policy (and social policy, more generally) relative to the fragmented and categorical approach adopted by the United States. Indeed, Kamerman and Kahn (2001) noted that the United States lacks a coherent family policy, that most policies that have implications for families and children are more implicit than explicit, and that U.S. policies “tend to be limited in scale, coverage, and generosity and are usually categorical and narrowly focused” (pp. 69–70). 

Because U.S. policy toward children and families is to a large extent implicit in nature, we believe it is particularly important to define such policy initiatives as family policy. Moreover, we contend that most social policy is, in essence, explicitly or implicitly family policy. For example, immigration policy and enforcement has clear implications for children’s living arrangements and associated household resources (Amuedo-Dorantes & Arenas-Arroyo, 2019)—with the recent child separation policy under the Trump administration on the U.S. southern border as the most extreme example. Criminal justice policy, including prisoner-reentry policy, also has a range of consequences for both parents and children, including setting the duration of time and geographic distance over which children are separated from incarcerated parents and the supports available to currently and formerly incarcerated individuals and their families (Turney, 2017; Western & Smith, 2018). Yet, neither of these is typically considered family policy. Defining policies in these and similar domains as implicit family policy recognizes that individuals are intrinsically linked throughout their life course—both within and across generations—by family relationships (Elder, 1994). This may be particularly important to take into account in an era of increasing family complexity, including multigenerational households and ongoing intra- and interhousehold transfers of resources across the life course (Wiemers, Seltzer, Schoeni, Hotz, & Bianchi, 2016). Finally, we contend that it is critical to label policies that have intended or unintended consequences for families or individuals in their family roles as “family policy” to bring families—and such consequences—to the forefront in research, policymaking, and policy practice. 

## Growing Family Complexity

In addition to considering what to include under the umbrella of “family policy,” it is also important to consider how we are defining and measuring families, especially as families have continued to evolve. Since the middle of the 20th century, a series of major family changes have occurred in the United States (and across most Western countries), including a delay and decline in marriage, an increase in cohabitation, a notable rise in divorce rates (followed by a decline in some nations), a high prevalence of repartnering (and associated multipartnered fertility), and a large increase in the share of births that occur outside of marriage (Furstenberg, 2014). Shared physical custody arrangements following parental union dissolution have also increased substantially in recent decades (and shared legal custody has been the norm for several decades; Cancian, Meyer, Brown, & Cook, 2014; D. R. Meyer, Cancian, & Cook, 2017). Union instability in the context of childbearing implies that individuals will experience multiple family forms with various dimensions of com plexity; adults are likely to spend time living with more than one partner, and children may experience changes in the adults with whom they coreside or who serve as parental figures in their lives. This means that families often span different households, that family members within households may not all share the same biological or relational ties, and that the involved individuals may have different definitions o who is “in” their family. Moreover, these factors are likely to differ over time. In addition, recen decades have seen large numbers and increased recognition of children living with their single or repartnered father without their biologica mother present in the household (Grall, 2013; Livingston, 2013), children living in households that include one or more grandparents (and may or may not include a biological parent, Dunifon, Ziol-Guest, & Kopko, 2014), children in “mixed” immigration-status families (Capps, Fix, & Zong, 2016; National Academies of Science, Engineering and Medicine, 2019), and children being raised by same-sex couples (Miller & Price, 2014). Together, these trends have resulted in growing uncertainty about family forms, relationships, and stability therein (Seltzer, 2019). They have also raised questions about “what types of families the government should support” (Cherlin, 2019, p. 48) 

Amidst this complexity, existing research and policy definitions of families may be inadequate. Most surveys that describe families use household-based measures that may underestimate or mischaracterize the full array of family ties and obligations, particularly among disadvantaged families. For example, the def inition of family still used by the U.S. Census Bureau is: “a group of two people or more (one of whom is the householder) related by birth, marriage, or adoption and residing together” (U.S. Census Bureau, 2019). This definition does not accurately characterize many families today for at least two reasons. First, it ignores cohabitation despite the fact that many individuals today are likely to cohabit and that a growing fraction of births occur to cohabiting couples; during the 2010 to 2014 period, fully 26% of births were to women in cohabiting relationships compared with 15% to single (unpartnered) women (Wu, 2014). Second, it ignores any family ties that span households, which are increasingly common because of both family dissolution and multipartnered fertility. For example, when their parents split up, children are likely to live away from their biological father and from any half- (or step-) siblings that may result from his repartnering. At the same time, however, given rising rates of parental shared custody, they are also increasingly likely to spend time in—and to be considered a member of—multiple households. Furthermore, cross-sectional classifications of families, in general, will not capture the dynamic and fluid nature of families, especially with respect to living arrangements and resource-sharing. 

Family complexity has key implications for understanding the character and composition of, as well as resource-sharing within, families. This is especially salient in the United States, where a largely residual welfare state expects families to take primary and ongoing responsibility for the care and socialization of children. Amidst complex families, resources that would have historically been concentrated within a single nuclear household are now diffused across households. For example, men who have fathered children with more than one woman and do not live with some or all of their children often do not have the economic resources to provide significant levels of support to all of these families (Cancian & Meyer, 2011; Sinkewicz & Garfinkel, 2009). Moreover, many individuals are likely to simultaneously occupy multiple partner and parent roles, and family complexity portends greater ambiguity and identity conflicts with respect to family roles and responsibilities (Berger & Bzostek, 2014). For example, scholars have described the role of stepparent with respect to parenting responsibilities as being rife with ambiguity for both parents and children (Cherlin & Furstenberg, 1994) and documents that children in stepfather families may not receive the level of emotional support that they need from either their nonresidential biological father or their stepfather (Cherlin, 1992; Stewart, 2006); at the same time, some research suggests that at least at one poin in time, married stepfathers can be as involved with children as biological fathers, although these families’ economic resources are, on average, far lower (Carlson & Berger, 2013). Finally, complexity may mean greater fluidity in living arrangements, as romantic partners of one parent move in and out of children’s lives. Because family complexity is most concentrated among disadvantaged populations—in concert with evidence that complex family arrangements may further diminish families’ abilities to support their children—growing family complexity may contribute to broader societal inequality both within and across generations (Seltzer, 2019). As such, family complexity represents an important aspect of the “diverging destinies” for children across different socioeconomic groups (McLanahan, 2004; McLanahan & Jacobsen, 2014). 

Family complexity is of particular concern with respect to public policy for at least two reasons. First, program eligibility and benefit determination depend on accurately assessing the composition, size, living arrangements, and resources of individuals and families, whereas growing complexity (and uncertainty) implies that categories may not be as discrete or as stable as assumed, even during the very short term. For example, the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) is paid to parents with low earnings who have custody of children (more than 6 months of the year), yet many parents today are equally or near-equally sharing custody of children after separation; the EITC benefit can not be apportioned across two tax filers. Second, complexity creates challenges and dilemmas of fairly adjudicating across individuals who share family ties but whose interests and incentives may diverge. For example, in the context of multiple-partner fertility, assessment of child support orders becomes much more complicated than in the context of single-partner fertility; indeed, prioritizing the needs of mothers, fathers or children may yield quite different approaches (Cancian & Meyer, 2011), and balancing equity, adequacy, and affordability concerns fo each of these actors has become increasingly difficult. 

## Family Policy in the United States: Trends, Developments, and Research Findings

A complete review of the detailed family policy developments in the past decade and research thereon is beyond the scope of a single article. As such, we begin with a review of the most salient ongoing policy trends and research of the past decade. We note that this period included the 50th anniversary of the War on Poverty and the 20th anniversary of the Personal Responsibility and Work Opportunity Reconciliation Act of 1996 (PRWORA); thus, we include recent research relating to these policies. We then highlight a subset of key recent policy developments that are likely to have substantial explicit or implicit impacts on children and families. The explicit policies include family leave, marriage equality, home visiting, contraceptive access, marriage promotion and relationship building, child welfare, child support enforcement, and early childhood education and care. The implicit policies include tax and health care policy. Where possible, we review research pertaining to each. ## Ongoing Policy Trends and Research Findings

In contrast to that in most other developed countries, explicit family policy in the United States is primarily targeted toward low-income families and determined by means-testing. Moreover, the share of federal expenditures (including tax expenditures) on children that is means tested has grown considerably over time from 15% in 1960, to 34% in 1970, and to fully 61% in 2017 (Isaacs et al., 2018). This stands in stark contrast to many other industrialized countries, which pursue a more universalistic approach to family policymaking, as well as to evidence suggesting that means-tested and targeted benefits are less effective for reducing poverty and inequality than universal social insurance benefits (Korpi & Palme, 1998). Cross-nationally, the Scandinavian countries typically offer more universal and generous welfare policies that provide higher levels of support and allow parents to better balance work and family obligations compared to the Anglo/English-speaking countries (espe cially the United States), which offer minima supports; central and southern European countries fall somewhere in between (Gornick, Meyers, & Ross, 1997). Indeed, a recent study of 24 Organisation for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) countries found that, although market-income poverty rates for single-parent families in the United States are on par with those of other countries, U.S. policies do far less to reduce market-income poverty, leaving the United States with the highest dis posable (posttax and transfer) income poverty rate (Maldonado & Nieuwenhuis, 2015). 

As noted previously, the past decade marked both the 50th anniversary of the War on Poverty, which was first introduced in Lyndon Johnson’s 1964 State of the Union Address, and the 20th anniversary of PRWORA. These anniversaries sparked additional research and reviews focus ing on trends in poverty in the aftermath of each. The dramatic expansion in social and family policy targeted at low-income populations that occurred in the 1960s was largely followed by incremental policy changes (with the notable exception of the establishment of the EITC and the child support enforcement system) unti PRWORA’s passage in 1996 (Berger, Cancian, & Maguson, 2018). Throughout that period, however, government spending and program participation (caseloads) consistently increased (Moffitt, 2015). 

Have social policies reduced poverty in the 5 decades since the beginning of the War on Poverty? A growing body of evidence suggests that, when in-kind benefits, such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), and tax transfers, such as the EITC (which now comprise the core of the U.S. safety net), are taken into account, poverty has been reduced substantially—to the tune of 40%—during the past 50 years (Fox, Wimer, Garfinkel, Kaushal, & Waldfogel, 2015; Haveman, Blank, Moffitt, Smeeding, & Wallace, 2015; Wimer, Fox, Garfinkel, Kaushal, & Waldfogel, 2016). More over, poverty among young children (ages 0–5) has decreased by almost a third, as a direct result of antipoverty programs, which play a particularly large role in lifting Black, non-Hispanic children out of poverty (Pac, Nam, Waldfogel, & Wimer, 2017). In short, at least with respect to reducing income poverty, research in the past decade makes a compelling case that social policy has been quite effective; for comprehensive reviews of the effects of social policy in the decades since the War on Poverty, see Bailey and Danziger (2013), Bitler and Karoly (2015), and National Academies of Science, Engineering and Medicine (2019). (Notably, this literature does not specifically focus on War on Poverty programs per se but, rather, on the full array of policies and programs in effect, including those existing prior to the War on Poverty, some of which were expanded during or subsequent to the War on Poverty, as well policies and programs enacted in more recent decades.) Beyond reducing poverty, Ooms (2019), in a recent review of family policy during the past 50 years, concludes that family policy has made “substantial progress” in “improving children’s and families’ lives” (although she further notes that these gains have “largely been unrecognized and are greatly undervalued by academic disciplines, policymakers, the media, and the general public,” p. 19).

Of additional note, during the past decade, the U.S. Census Bureau began (in 2011) annually releasing supplemental poverty measure (SPM) estimates in addition to official poverty measure (OPM) estimates. Among other things, the SPM accounts for near cash in-kind benefits and tax transfers that are not accounted for by the OPM. Although the SPM is better suited for measuring the economic effects of social welfare policies, it is not currently used for program eligibility determination. A detailed discussion of poverty measurement, including differences between the OPM and SPM is available in the recent report from the National Academies of Science, Engineering and Medicine focused on reducing child poverty (National Academies of Science, Engineering and Medicine, 2019). 

PRWORA signaled a significant shift in policy toward low-income families by ending the entitlement to unconditional means-tested cash assistance in favor of time-limited benefits conditioned with work requirements, as well as by decoupling food stamps and Medicaid from cash welfare. In concert with considerable expansions in child-care subsidies and the EITC, it also signaled a shift away from direct cash assistance in favor of in-kind assistance and indirect cash assistance through the tax system. Since its 1996 passage, the real value of Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF) 

cash benefits has declined substantially (Burn side & Floyd, 2019). Furthermore, whereas cash transfers accounted for the vast majority of social welfare support to families with young children in the late 1960s, today’s benefit pack age to such families consists of equal parts cash, in-kind, and tax (credit) benefits (Pac et al., 2017). Specifically, in-kind assistance through SNAP and refundable tax credits (EITC and the refundable portion of the child tax credit [CTC]) now constitute the largest and most important income support programs for low-income children and families in the United States (Hardy, Smeeding, & Ziliak, 2018). Indeed, Isaacs et al. (2018) report that the majority of federal spend ing on children is currently through the EITC ($60 billion), the CTC ($49 billion), and the Dependent Exemption ($38 billion), with the children’s share of SNAP and TANF funding totaling $31 billion and $13 billion, respectively. The federal government also spends considerably on children’s health, most prominently through Medicaid ($90 billion) and the Chil dren’s Health Insurance Program ($15 billion). 

These policy changes have resulted in relatively less assistance being targeted to nonelderly nondisabled families, single-parent families, nonworking families, and the poorest families, and relatively more assistance targeted to the elderly and disabled, married families, working families, and less-poor (but still low-income) families (Haveman et al., 2015; Moffitt, 2015), trends that continued through the past decade. Current proposals and state waiver applications to expand the work requirements in SNAP and to introduce them in Medicaid indicate that these trends are ongoing. Yet, despite large declines in TANF caseloads in the wake of welfare reform—and the common perception that the U.S. social welfare system has been contracting—overall social welfare and family policy spending has, for the most part, continued to steadily grow during the past half century (Moffitt, 2015). Indeed, direct spending on children grew from 3.2% of the federal budget in 1960 to peak at 10.6% in 2010 before declining to 9.4% or $325 billion in 2017, and an additional $106 billion in fed eral tax expenditures is targeted to families with children (Isaacs et al., 2018). State and local governments spend considerably more, accounting for roughly two-thirds of all public expenditures on children. 

Still, the changes spurred by PRWORA and subsequent policies have had substantial implications for low-income families. For example, while unemployment rose sharply during the economic downturn of the Great Recession, leading to a considerable increase in the SNAP caseload, TANF participation did not grow accordingly. As such, poverty trends appear to be more closely tied to market cycles than in the past (Bitler & Hoynes, 2016). Consistent with this finding, in their review of the evidence on welfare reform, Danziger, Danziger, Seefeldt, and Shaefer (2016) conclude that TANF more successfully assisted low-income families during the economic boom of the 1990s than during the high unemployment period of the Great Recession. Moreover, they highlight considerable heterogeneity in families economic situations under “the new safety net” (p. 4), such that families that are able to maintain employment are financially better off, on average, whereas those with barriers to stable employment—health and mental health problems and limited labor market skills and experience—are worse off. 

Declines in cash assistance to low-income nonworking families sparked an interest in deep or extreme poverty in the United States in the decades following welfare reform. Edin and Shaefer (2015) and Shaefer, Edin, and Talbert (2015) focused their work on those subsisting on $2 a day per person in cash income within the United States, finding extreme disadvantage among a subset of the U.S. population. The extremes of U.S. poverty were also highlighted in a recent report of the U.N. Human Rights Council Special Rapporteur on extreme poverty and human rights (Human Rights Council, 2018); the report highlighted the “stark contrasts” in the United States, with both immense wealth and millions living in “Third World conditions of absolute poverty” (p. 3). Although there is considerable debate about the size and scope of the deep poor population and trends in deep poverty over time, with estimates varying widely (Edin & Shaefer, 2015; Fox et al., 2015; Meyer, Mooers, & Wu, 2018; Shaefer et al., 2015; Shaefer & Edin, 2013; Winship, 2016), given the shift away from cash support in the United States during the past 2 or more decades, it is reasonable to conclude that a nonnegligible number of Americans live on very little cash income. It is also clear that counting SNAP and other in-kind benefits as income equivalents substantially reduces the estimates of the depth and severity of poverty (Meyer & Wu, 2018; Shaefer & Edin, 2013; Tiehen, Jolliffe, & Smeeding, 2016). 

Finally, although states have always had considerable flexibility in policy design and administration, especially with respect to cash welfare, the 1996 reforms facilitated a substantial increase in state policy variation, particularly for programs affected by PRWORA (Bruch, Meyers, & Gornick, 2018). This trend has also continued throughout the decade in a wide range of explicit and implicit family policy domains, including state Medicaid (Buettgens, Holahan, & Recht, 2015; Courtemanche, Mar ton, Ukert, Yelowtiz, & Zapata, 2016; Rose, 2015) and EITC expansions (Cooper, Lutz, & Palumbo, 2015; Williams, 2015), cash benefits (Schott, Pavetti, & Floyd, 2015; Stanley & Floyd, 2016), prekindergarten programs (Bartik & Hershbein, 2018; Friedman-Krauss, Barnett, & Nores, 2016), and paid family leave (Isaacs, Healy, & Peters, 2017). 

In contrast to many of these ongoing trends, however, the American Reinvestment and Recovery Act of 2009, which was enacted in response to the Great Recession, temporarily (but substantially) expanded eligibility and ben efit generosity, particularly for the EITC, SNAP, and unemployment insurance programs. It also included temporary changes in the CTC, income and payroll taxes, and the Supplemental Secu rity Income program as well as additional TANF funds (Moffitt, 2015). (Notably, the temporary EITC and CTC expansions under the American Reinvestment and Recovery Act of 2009 were made permanent in 2015.) As such, Bitler, Hoynes, and Kuka (2017) document that, after accounting for taxes and in-kind benefits, the safety net substantially reduced private-income poverty during the Great Recession (although noting that TANF was less responsive to the recession than the EITC, SNAP, and unemploy ment insurance). However, they also document considerable variation by demographic groups such that immigrant families and the most disadvantaged families were less well supported than their U.S.-born and more-advantaged counterparts. Next, we review what we view as the most salient explicit and implicit developments in family policy of the past decade. 

## Recent Explicit Family Policy Developments

Major explicit family policy developments during the past decade have occurred in a range of areas, most notably family leave, marriage equality, home visiting, contraceptive choice and availability, marriage promotion and relationship building, child welfare, child support enforcement, and early childhood education and care. We briefly review these developments and, where relevant, the research to date thereon. 

## State Family Leave Policies

Unlike its peer nations, the United States has no national paid family leave program. During the past decade, however, there has been considerable policy development at the state level to enact paid family leave, and a recent American Enterprise Institute–Brookings working group has called for a national paid family and medical leave policy (American Enterprise Institute–Brookings, 2017). In 2002, California became the first state to adopt a statewide paid family leave policy, which went into effect in 2004. It was followed by New Jersey (enacted 2008, effective 2009), Rhode Island (2013, 2014), New York (2016, 2018), and Washington, D.C. (2017, 2020). Paid leave periods range from 4 to 8 weeks and from 50% to 90% wage replacement up to a maximum weekly benefit ranging from less than $200 to more than $1,000 per week. All of these policies are less generous than those of other OECD countries, which offer new mothers an average of 54 weeks of leave and new fathers an average of 8 weeks of leave with wage replacement rates varying from 20% to100% (American Enterprise Institute–Brookings, 2017); yet they represent significant policy expansions. 

Given that these policies have been enacted so recently, research to date has examined only the effects of the California policy. Results from California indicate that the policy served to increase the use and average length of maternity leave, particularly for less-advantaged families, as well as to increase work hours and wages for working mothers of young children (Rossin-Slater, Ruhm, & Waldfogel, 2013). It also increased leave-taking among fathers in dual-earner households, particularly in association with first births (Bartel, Rossin-Slater, 

Ruhm, Stearns, & Waldfogel, 2018). Effects on children have also been quite positive, with analyses revealing declines in infant hospitalizations for reasons potentially associated with the caregiving environment (Pihl & Basso, 2019) and improved elementary school health, particularly for less-advantaged children (Lichtman-Sadot & Bell, 2017). In addition, the policy significantly reduced the proportion of the elderly that used nursing homes, as employees can take time off to provide informal care for elders (Arora & Wolf, 2018). 

## Marriage Equality (Same-Sex Marriage)

The U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) that same-sex couples have a constitutional right to marry. As such, all states are now required to offer same-sex couples legal marriages with the same parameters as those offered to opposite-sex couples. The research literature on the effects of same-sex marriage on adults and children is still in its early stages, and challenges exist in terms of sample sizes, identification of individuals in same sex-couples, study recruitment, heterogeneity by gender and sexual identity, and identification of appropriate comparison groups and counterfactuals (Umberson, Thomeer, Kroeger, Lodge, & Xu, 2015). However, the research to date indicates that access to legal same-sex marriage has led to increases in marriage among both men and women in same-sex relationships (Carpenter, Eppink, Gonzales, & McKay, 2018). It has also resulted in higher levels of self-assessed health (Kail, Acosta, & Wright, 2015), decreased psychological stress, and increased life satisfaction among members of same-sex couples (Ogolsky, Monk, Rice, & Oswald, 2019), as well as greater access to health insurance and health care and greater health care utilization, particularly for gay men (Carpenter et al., 2018). Furthermore, although not explicitly focusing on same-sex marriage, recent reviews of the research on the development and well-being of children raised by same-sex parents indicates that, all else equal, these children fare similarly to those raised by different-sex parents across a wide range of developmental domains, and that any observed differences reflect differences in family stability and socioeconomic status (Manning, Fettro, & Lamidi, 2014). 

## Home Visiting and Maternal and Child Health Services

In light of a growing body of research pointing to the effectiveness of high-quality evidence-based home visiting programs (see Michalopoulos, Faucetta, Warren, & Mitchell, 2017; Sama-Miller et al., 2018) on a range of outcomes throughout the life course, the Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act of 2010 (ACA) authorized the Maternal, Infant, and Early Childhood Home Visiting Program. Specifically, the Act provided $1.5 billion in federal funding over 5 years for state and local implementation to expand access to evidence-based home visiting for disadvantaged children. The legislation also requires ongoing research and evaluation, including funding a Home Visiting Research Network. The Maternal, Infant, and Early Childhood Home Visiting Program grants represent a large-scale federal investment in the health, development, and well-being of disadvantaged young children. The initiative requires grantees to implement one or more of the 20 home visiting models (as of August 2019) that have demonstrated evidence of effectiveness (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2019). Grantees must evaluate program outcomes in areas such as maternal and child health, child maltreatment and injury, school readiness and achievement, crime and domestic violence, economic self-sufficiency, and coordination and referrals for community resources and supports. Of particular note, the initiative targets at-risk communities rather than means-testing individuals. Recipient states are required to continue to fund existing home visiting programs at their current spending levels (Adirim & Supplee, 2013). The program was most recently reauthorized under the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018, which appropriated $400 million per year through fiscal year 2022 to the program. 

The Mother and Infant Home Visiting Program Evaluation (MIHOPE), launched by the Department of Health and Human Services in 2011, focuses on the impacts of four evidence-based home visiting models being implemented in 12 states and 88 local programs with a sample of more than 4,000 pregnant women or families with children younger than 6 months of age. Interim results, when sample children were approximately 15 months of age, suggest significant positive results on four of 12 outcomes assessed—better quality of the home environment and less psychological aggression toward the child, fewer emergency room visits, and fewer child behavioral problems. A companion study to MIHOPE, MIHOPE-Strong Start, is evaluating two of the interventions for their influence on prenatal, maternal and infant health in 17 states and 66 local programs with a sample of nearly 3,000 women recruited during the first 32 weeks of pregnancy. Interim results, when sample children were approximately 15 months of age, indicate little influence of MIHOPE-Strong Start on prenatal behaviors or pregnancy outcomes (Michalopoulos et al., 2019). 

## Contraceptive Choice and Availability

As detailed by Sawhill (2014), evidence suggests that widespread and easily available access to long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) has considerable potential to substantially reduce unplanned pregnancy and associated family complexity. LARCs have lower rates of failure and noncompliant use than other forms of reversible contraception, and they are more effective at preventing pregnancy. Yet, LARC usage rates have been relatively low in the United States, particularly for disadvantaged women, given limited infor mation among both providers and potential users, a traditionally multiappointment process for insertion, and high up-front (although not necessarily long-term) costs. However, a growing body of evidence suggests that providing counseling at family planning clinics to explain how LARCs work and making them available for free could result in substantial increases in their use and a resulting decrease in unplanned births (McNicholas, Madden, Secura, & Peipert, 2014; Ricketts, Klingler, & Schwalberg, 2014). This evidence has driven considerable policy development at the federal and state level. At the federal level, the ACA mandates that health insurance cover all U.S. Food and Drug Administration–approved contraceptive methods, including LARCs, at no cost to patients. At the state level, seven states (Delaware, New Mexico, Tennessee, Utah, Vermont, Virginia, West Virginia) and Washington, D.C. now have LARC-related policies such as requiring insurance companies or Medicaid to provide access to postpartum LARC insertion or health care providers to provide information about LARCs or otherwise improve access (National Conference of State Legislators, 2018). 

Delaware was the first state to engage in a statewide initiative to provide easy access to contraceptive methods of choice to all women in the state. To date, evidence suggests a more than doubling of LARC usage; simulations suggest that these changes could result in a 15% decline in unintended pregnancies (Welti & Manlove, 2018). Similar initiatives have recently been launched in Massachusetts, North Carolina, and Washington. It is important to recognize, however, that there is also some evidence that clinicians may encourage women of color, more so than White women, to engage in LARC usage, raising important concerns about reproductive social justice (Dehlendorf et al., 2010; Higgins, Kramer, & Ryder, 2016) and highlighting the need for patient-centered care in contraceptive counseling to ensure women’s autonomy in reproductive health decisions (Higgins, 2014). 

## Marriage Promotion and Relationship Building

PRWORA’s explicit goal of promoting marriage and increasing the number of children raised in two-parent families, coupled with subsequent legislation in 2005 and 2010, provided funding for the implementation and evaluation of programs intended to promote healthy marriage and responsible fatherhood. The large-scale Building Strong Families healthy marriage and relationship education program targeting unmarried expecting or recent parents is perhaps the largest and most well-known project. On the whole, findings have not been promising. Multisite Building Strong Families randomized controlled trial (RCT) results indicate no overall program effects on relationship quality, relationship stability, or marriage (though there was some heterogeneity in results by program site) (Wood, McConnell, Moore, Clarkwest, & Hsueh, 2012). Specifically, at the 3-year follow-up, there was no evidence of improved couple relationships, coparenting quality, or father involvement as well as no effect on family stability or economic resources for children. There were, however, some modest positive effects on child socioemotional development (Wood, Moore, Clarkwest, & Killewald, 2014). Additional projects focused on unmarried parents were launched in 2015, and evaluations are ongoing. 

Another intervention, the Supporting Healthy Marriage program, which focused on enhancing relationship quality and increasing marital stability among low-income married couples with children, was launched in 2003, and the final evaluation was released in 2014. The yearlong marriage education program was delivered at eight sites. It included group workshops using structured relationship skills curricula along with additional activities and family support services to reinforce key themes and connect families with other services. The 30-month evaluation (Lundquist et al., 2014) showed that Supporting Healthy Marriage did not increase marital stability and did not influence measures of coparenting, parenting, or child well-being; however, the program did produce sustained positive effects on aspects of couple relationship quality, including higher marital happiness, warmth, and effective communication along with lower levels of marital distress, infidelity, and hostile behaviors. 

Most recently, the Parents and Children Together evaluation assessed the impact of four responsible fatherhood and two healthy marriage programs. The results are somewhat more promising than those from earlier evaluations. For example, the responsible fatherhood programs improved fathers’ (self-reported) parenting behaviors and increased the average length of time fathers were continuously employed. At the same time, they had no impacts on parents’ coparenting quality, fathers’ earn ings, or fathers’ social–emotional well-being (Avellar, Covington, Moore, Patnaik, & Wu, 2018). The healthy marriage programs improved couples’ sense of commitment toward their relationship, sense of support and affection toward each other, and coparenting quality; they also slightly increased the likelihood that couples remained married after 1 year, but did not increase marriage among initially unmarried couples or consistently improve economic well-being (Moore, Avellar, Patnaik, Covington, & Wu, 2018). 

A recent meta-analysis of existing responsi ble fatherhood program evaluations (Holmes, Hawkins, Egginton, Robbins, & Shafer, 2018) concluded that, on average, such programs were associated with small but statistically significant increases in father involvement (father–child contact), parenting skills, and coparenting. No significant effects were found on father employment, economic well-being, or child support payment. Looking toward the future, the ongoing Building Bridges and Bonds evaluation (Office of Planning, Research and Evaluation/U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2019), spanning the years 2014 to 2021, will provide evidence on the impact of six fatherhood programs using cognitive-behavioral, play-based, and smart phone (app)-based approaches. Key outcomes include father–child contact, father–child relationship quality, and fathers’ employment, earnings, and child support payment. 

## Child Welfare

Federal child welfare funding has long been primarily devoted to children in out-of-home care (OHC) rather than the prevention of child maltreatment and associated out-of-home placement. Given growing evidence that high-quality prevention programs can reduce child abuse and neglect and thereby OHC, scholars and policymakers have increasingly called for child welfare financing reforms to expandstate and local flexibility to provide prevention services; for a review of these issues, see Haskins, Currie, and Berger (2015). The Family First Prevention and Services Act (FFPSA), included in the Bipartisan Budget Act of 2018, has the potential to radically change the child welfare services landscape. Under the Act, for the first time, states are able to use federal Title IV-E child welfare funding for trauma-informed evidence-based child maltreatment prevention efforts in addition to foster care services. Services can be provided to children and families at risk of OHC for up to 12 months and include mental health, substance abuse, and parenting interventions. The Act also removes time limits for family reunification services funded by the Promoting Safe and Stable Families program, which will allow families a longer time period to work toward family reunification following OHC placement; this may be especially important for those struggling with opioid and other substance addiction. In addi tion, the FFPSA limits placements in congregate care (group homes) except in the most necessary situations, supporting the longstanding policy preference that children be raised in the most family-like environments possible (Institute for Research on Poverty, 2018). States are currently in the process of developing implementation plans. 

## Child Support Enforcement

Although no major child support legislation was passed in the past decade, an executive order was implemented to enhance “flexibility, effi ciency and modernization” in child support programs (Office of Child Support Enforcement [OSCE], 2016). New rules under this order are focused on allowing more flexible determina tion of child support obligations based on non custodial parents’ abilities to pay, incorporating technological advances, and reducing the accumulation of unpaid and uncollectible arrears. In addition, states can no longer consider incar ceration to be “voluntary unemployment” for the purposes of assessing child support obligations. These changes were motivated by a growing body of research indicating that child support orders tend to be relatively unrespon sive to changes in noncustodial parents’ incomes (Ha, Cancian, & Meyer, 2010) and that reducing child support debt can lead to increased non custodial parent employment and child support payment (Cancian, Heinrich, & Chung, 2013; Heinrich, Burkhardt, & Shager, 2011). 

There has also been growing interest during the past decade in reforming the child support enforcement system to better assist noncustodial parents in finding and keeping employment and increasing earnings to better facilitate child support transfers to children (Couch, 2010; Mead, 2010a, 2010b; Sorensen, 2010a, 2010b). As such, the federal government launched, in 2012, the National Child Support Noncustodial Parent Employment Demonstration (CSPED), an eight-state pilot program that aimed to test the efficacy of providing noncustodial parents with integrated case management, enhanced child support services including review and adjustment of orders, employment services, and fatherhood and parenting services to increase the reliable payment of child support. Maria Cancian and Daniel Meyer led a randomized evaluation of the CSPED initiative using a sample of more than 10,000 noncustodial parents. On the whole, the intervention led to modest decreases in child support order amounts, which were better aligned with noncustodial parents earnings, and smaller reductions in child sup port payments, but no changes in child support compliance. Moreover, although CSPED did not increase employment, it resulted in some (small) increases in noncustodial parents’ earnings. 

In addition, noncustodial parents in the treat ment group reported much improved attitudes toward the child support program, greater sense of responsibility toward their children, and increased contact with nonresident children (Cancian, Meyer, & Wood, 2019). 

## Early Childhood Education and Care

A large body of research has established that early childhood is a crucial developmental stage and that high-quality early education and care programs have the potential to improve child development and well-being, particularly for disadvantaged children; such efforts could thereby reduce socioeconomic disparities in school readiness, with implications for intergenerational transmission of inequality and social mobility; for an extensive review, see Elango, Garcia, Heckman, and Holman (2016). As noted previously, PRWORA and subsequent policies resulted in a significant increase in public investment in child care. The Child Care and Development Block Grant Act of 2014 focused on increasing access to and improving the quality of child care targeted to low-income families. Specifically, the Act increased parental choice in child-care arrangements while seeking to improve subsidy stability and continuity of care through more family-friendly eligibility and redetermination procedures and transparent consumer (and provider) education materials. It also strengthened health and safety requirements for providers and further sought to improve the quality of child-care and early childhood services, as well as the workforce delivering those services, by increasing the proportion of funds states must commit to high-quality providers and requiring ongoing professional development and training activities. 

## Recent Implicit Family Policy Developments

Additional recent developments in tax and health care policy, both of which we view as implicit family policy, may have important implications for families. We review these next. 

## Tax Policy

The major U.S. tax policy overhaul under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 (TCJA) has a wide range of implications for families. Key family relevant provisions include: (a) reducing the marginal tax rate for all but the lowest income filers (those with a 10% rate); (b) nearly doubling the standard deduction from $13,000 to $24,000 for married families filing jointly, $6,500 to $12,000 for single filers, and $9,500 to $18,000 for heads of households, but repealing the individual deduction (formerly $4,050; notably, the value of the standard deduction increases with a filer’s marginal tax rate such that it is worth more to higher income filers; also, because it is nonrefundable, it is of no value to those who do not owe taxes); (c) increasing the CTC to $2,000 per child and its refundable portion to $1,400 (the refundable portion is calculated at 15% of wages above $2,500), as well as phasing the credit out at a higher income level (these provisions serve to increase both the value of the credit and the number of families who can claim it; however, the credit is not indexed for inflation and will thus decline in real value over time); (d) reducing the cap on the mortgage interest deduction to interest debt paid on a maximum of $750,000 (from $1 million) in acquisition debt for a first or second home purchase and eliminating the deduction for home equity debt; (e) capping the state tax deduction at $10,000; and (f) reducing the ACA-mandated tax penalty for not having health insurance to $0. However, with the exception of the elimination of the ACA penalty, these provisions are set to expire after 2025; see Gale, Gelfond, Krupkin, Mazur, and Toder (2018) for a complete summary of the TCJA. 

Analyses of the potential effects of the TCJA suggest that, on the whole, all income groups and family types were expected to experience tax reductions in 2018, with an average increase of 1.6% in after-tax income. However, the largest reductions were targeted to higher income groups. Considering all TCJA provisions, on average, in 2018, filers in the bottom quintile were expected to experience a $60 reduction in federal taxes, those in the middle quintile a $930 reduction, and those in the top quintile a $7,640 reduction, representing tax decreases of 0.4%, 1.4%, and 2.2%, respectively. Moreover, although nearly 65% of taxpayers were expected to receive a tax cut, this was true for only 27% of filers in the lowest income quintile compared with 82% of middle-income quintile filers and 90% of top-income quintile filers (Sammartino, Stallworth, & Weiner, 2018). Furthermore, those in the lowest income quintile were expected to realize a roughly 0.4% increase in total after-tax income, those in the middle quintile a 1.5% increase, and those in the top 1% a 2.5% increase—and these disparities are expected to widen over time (Tax Policy Center, 2017a). 

There is also considerable heterogeneity in effects by marital status and family size, as well as across states (Sammartino et al., 2018). Most notably, tax reductions are expected to be generally larger for lower earning families with fewer children and for higher earning families with more children (Tax Policy Center, 2017b). On the whole, the Joint Committee on Taxation and the Congressional Budget Office estimate that the TCJA will result in decreased federal revenue of $1.5 trillion by 2027. If accurate, a loss of federal revenue of this magnitude could have implications for family policy expenditures in the future. 

Beyond the TCJA, the past decade saw a range of policy developments with respect to state EITCs. To date, 26 states offer a state EITC, which is usually calculated as a percentage of the federal EITC. Since 2009, one state (California) enacted an EITC, 13 states expanded their EITC, and four states contracted their EITC (Connecticut passed legislation both expanding and contracting its EITC during the course of the decade). As noted previously, there is clear evidence that EITCs promote work and decrease poverty and economic hardship. There is also a growing body of research indicating that they are associated with improved health and well-being in a wide range of domains for both children and adults (Nichols & Rothstein, 2016). 

## Health Care

The ACA has several provisions that are particularly relevant for families. In addition to the home visiting provisions discussed previously, these include providing premium subsidies for those with incomes between 100% and 400% of poverty, expanding eligibility for Medicaid to 138% of poverty (as of August 2019, 36 states and Washington, DC, have expanded their Medicaid programs [Henry J. Kaiser Family Foundation, 2019]), requiring private plans to provide contraception benefits, and requiring parents’ policies to allow coverage of their children through age 26. Research to date on the ACA indicates that state Medicaid expansions are associated with considerable increases in health care coverage, particularly for (childless) young adults and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations (Courtemanche, Marton, Ukert, Yelowitz, & Zapata, 2017), as well as with increased use of preventive care and improvements in self-assessed health (Simon, Soni, & Cawley, 2017). Research also indicates that the contraception coverage mandate served to increase contraception use and decrease abortion (Mulligan, 2015). In addition, the provisions allowing young adults to remain on their parents’ health policies until age 26, coupled with the contraception coverage requirement, are associated with a modest decrease in fertility among young adults (Heim, Lurie, & Simon, 2018) however, there appears to be no association of Medicaid expansions with fertility (DeLeire, Lopoo, & Simon, 2011). 

## Family Complexity and Family Policy

We next consider recent changes in families and their implications for policy. First, we briefly summarize six key family patterns about which new research has emerged in the past decade, and which have important implications for ongoing public policy challenges in meeting the needs of children and parents living in varied and dynamic circumstances. Key patterns, here, include multipartnered fertility; shared custody; grandparent-headed and multigenerational households; single-father families; same-sex parent families; and immigrant, mixed-status, and undocumented families. We also highlight increasing inequality between families as a key overarching pattern. Subsequently, we consider how well current family policies and programs serve complex contemporary families. 

## Recent Family Patterns

## Multipartnered Fertility (MPF)

The past decade witnessed a burgeoning of research on multipartnered fertility, as new analytic approaches and data sources brought additional evidence about the nature and implications of the phenomenon of parents having children by multiple partners. A special issue focused on family complexity, poverty, and public policy (Carlson & Meyer, 2014) included articles that developed new estimates of family complexity from the perspective of children (Manning, Brown, & Stykes, 2014), young adults (Berger & Bzostek, 2014), and men and women (Guzzo, 2014) and also highlighted some of the chal lenges for mothers (Burton, 2014) and fathers (Tach, Edin, Harvey, & Bryan, 2014). Several recent articles have highlighted key issues regarding measuring MPF, especially differences across data sources and whether estimates are focused on mothers, fathers, or children (Guzzo & Dorius, 2016; Stykes & Guzzo, 2019). In addition, the 2014 Survey of Income and Program Participation was the first national U.S. survey to directly ask and measure whether individuals have had children by more than one partner. Overall, 10.1% of those aged 15 and older have children by more than one partner, and this is true of 15.7% of parents (Monte, 2017); MPF is more common among racial/ethnic minorities, those with low education, and those who are unmarried (Monte, 2019). 

MPF is salient to family policy because, by definition, it creates family complexity and typically results in family members spanning multiple households, which makes benefit eligibility and resource allocation difficult. As discussed later in this article, most existing policies were designed under the assumption of single-partner fertility such that family units consist only of adults and their joint children and, hence, benefit from economies of scale with respect to the cost of raising children. Yet, in the context of MPF, such that parents have children with multiple partners and these children live in multiple households, economies of scale are less applicable than in the single-partner fertility context. This raises technical and ideological issues for policy and program design regarding equity and adequacy across various family forms, which span areas such as income support, child support, tax policy, health care provision, and any other policies that require that individuals (particularly children) be identified as belonging to only a single family or household unit. Moreover, MPF is particularly salient with respect to child-conditioned transfers between parental households (child support policies), as it has widespread implications for the amounts of such and the extent to which they are affordable to noncustodial parents, adequate for children (and their custodial parents), and equitable to children of varying birth order or from varying relationships. 

## Shared Custody

Historically children have typically lived with their mothers (rather than fathers) following parental union dissolution, and most single-parent families continue to be headed by single mothers (Grall, 2013). At the same time, there has been a striking increase in shared custody (particularly after divorce) in the past 2 decades. Cancian et al. (2014) showed that between 1988 and 2008, the share of mothers granted sole custody in divorce cases in Wisconsin fell dramatically, whereas the share of parents sharing custody increased (and the share of fathers granted sole custody remained stable); Meyer et al. (2017) found that, by 2010, fully half of custody arrangements following divorce in Wisconsin were shared (where children spend at least 25% of time with each parent). Shared custody has especially important implications for tax and transfer programs that depend on identifying who lives in a household (or which of the parents has custody) to determine benefit eligibility and amounts. In particular, many policies and programs require that a child be claimed by only one parent and do not apply prorated eligibility or benefit determinations based on custodial time-sharing. That is, many benefits and tax credits cannot be shared across households when parents have joint custody. Thus, parents are forced to list only one custodial parent. As in the case of MPF, this raises affordability, adequacy, and equity concerns for balancing supports and obligations across parents and children with respect to both public and private transfers. 

## Grandparent-Headed and Multigenerational Households

The past decade saw growing awareness that many children live in households that include relatives beyond their nuclear families (parents and siblings), and may or may not include one o both parents. Although there is more work to be done to illuminate the nature and implications of these patterns, recent research shows that about one-fifth of children live in shared households with extended family members or nonrelatives. Grandparents are especially important in this regard: A nontrivial number of children are being raised by grandparents (Dunifon, 2018), and nearly 30% of U.S. children will live with their grandparent(s) at some point during child hood (Amorim, Dunifon, & Pilkauskas, 2017). Indeed, the proportion of children living in multigenerational households has increased dramatically during the past 2 decades, to 9.8% in 2016, compared to 5.7% in 1996 (Pilkauskas & Cross, 2018). By comparison, approximately 2.0% of children in 1996% and 2.2% of children in 2014 lived with their grandparent(s) without a parent present (U.S. Census, 2018). Moreover, such households are especially common among racial/ethnic minorities, unmarried and younger mothers, and families that are more economically disadvantaged (Pilkauskas, 2014), and these living arrangements are often short term with frequent transitions (Pilkauskas, 2012). They are also diverse in why they come about and how they influence members (Dunifon et al., 2014). Such family arrangements are important for public policy because kin (and especially coresidential kin) may be a source of support or need, affecting the household’s available resources through provision or consumption thereof. Yet, many programs and policies do not take into account that other relatives in a household may provide or command resources. Also, nonparental caregivers who have custodial responsibilities for children often lack the legal authority to obtain benefits that would otherwise be available to biological parents, or may have difficulty being recognized as decision-makers in other organizations such as schools or health care facilities. These issues are particularly salient for the large number of grandparents who have substantial or primary responsibility for rearing their grandchildren, either temporarily or permanently. 

## Single-Father Families

Although single-father families are considerably less common than single-mother families, the share of children living with their father, but not their mother, has increased in recent decades (Grall, 2013; Livingston, 2013). Only 1% of children in a single-father family in 1960, while this was true of 4% of all children in 2018—a rapid rate of increase but from a low base level; by comparison, 22% of children were living with a single mother in 2018 (U.S. Census, 2018). Single-father families typically have greater economic resources than single-mother families—but fewer economic resources than two-parent families. Single fathers are often reticent to apply for cash welfare assistance because of the stigma surrounding such and the belief that they should be able to provide for their children (Hahn, 2016). Moreover, although there are no explicit policy differences for children being raised by single fathers and single mothers, because single-father families have, on average, greater market-based economic resources, they are less likely to be eligible for means-tested benefits; also, many public services (e.g., home visiting programs) are primarily oriented around mothers and children and may be less “male friendly.” 

## Same-Sex Parent Families

Although children in same-sex parent families represent a small minority of children (Gates, 2012; Miller & Price, 2014), they have grown in numbers and in visibility. The best estimate to date indicates that roughly 240,000 children are being raised by a gay or lesbian parent and that roughly 70% of these are being raised by a same-sex couple (Miller & Price, 2014). As noted above, the Supreme Court legalized same-sex marriage. Moreover, attitudes toward same-sex couples and families have changed dramatically. However, other policy changes have been slower to evolve. For example, same-sex parents are sometimes prevented from adopting children, and policies have been partic ularly slow to respond to same-sex couple union dissolutions involving children. In particular, in cases in which married or cohabiting same-sex couples break up, but only one parent is the biological or adoptive parent of the child(ren), child support and visitation orders have been uneven, sometimes leading to protracted legal cases. 

## Immigrant, Mixed-Status, and Undocumented Families

Immigrant, undocumented, and mixed-status families are inherently complex vis-à-vis their interactions with public policies and programs, regardless of whether they are demographically complex, as defined by family composition (coresidential, biological, and marital ties). Approximately one quarter of U.S. children live in an “immigrant” family, in which one or both parents or some or all of the children were not born in the United States. The vast majority (nearly 91%) of these children are U.S. citizens, but 40% live with noncitizen parents (National Academies of Science, Engineering and Medicine, 2019). Moreover, about 7% of all U.S. children and 30% of children living in immigrant families live with at least one undocumented parent; 79% of these children are 

U.S. citizens, 2% have legal status, and 19% are undocumented themselves (Capps et al., 2016). 

Immigrant families, including those with undocumented members, are more socioeconomically disadvantaged and have higher poverty rates than nonimmigrant families, but are considerably less likely to be served by social welfare policies and programs (National Academies of Science, Engineering and Medicine, 2019). This reflects ineligibility due to undocumented or other immigration status (including, in many programs, ineligibility of otherwise income-eligible documented noncitizen immigrants, particularly more recent arrivals), insufficient tenure as a documented immigrant in the United States, and insufficient U.S. work history or military service. It also reflects low rates of program application among eligible immigrant families due to factors such as limited awareness of eligibility, complex eligibility requirements (and considerable variation therein across states) and administrative burden (Vargas & Pirog, 2016), and fear of deportation (Capps, Fix, Ost, Reardon-Anderson, & Passel, 2005) or adverse effects on future permanent residence or citizenship applications (Batalova, Fix, & Greenberg, 2018). Beyond eligibility and take-up, all else equal, mixed-status families participating in social welfare programs receive lesser benefit amounts because their undocumented or, for many programs, noncitizen members are ineligible and thus excluded from the benefit calculation (National Academies of Science, Engineering and Medicine, 2019). For all of these reasons, immigrant, undocumented, and mixed-status families represent key subgroups of the population that are not well served in the current policy and programmatic landscape. 

## Increasing Inequality Between Families

Finally, we turn to a cross-cutting issue that is salient for a range of families and is linked with the availability of public policy supports. Amidst rising economic inequality in the United States, there has been ongoing recognition that many of the family patterns observed in recent decades are also highly stratified by socioeconomic status. Extending McLanahan’s (2004) analyses of “diverging destinies” in children’s family experiences by maternal education, McLanahan and Jacobsen (2014) found, 10 years later, that divergent patterns in family formation had persisted, with important consequences for children’s well-being. Indeed, cohabitation, marriage, and childbearing behav iors are shown to differ by education, with the biggest difference observed between those with college degrees versus those with less education (Lundberg et al., 2016). Similarly, parenting behaviors also diverge between advantaged and disadvantaged parents, contributing to achievement gaps among children (Kalil, 2015). These patterns are important for policy because they highlight the scope of needs among disadvantaged families—those with the fewest economic resources are also those with the least stable (and most uncertain) family situations, further diluting the resources that exist (Seltzer, 2019). From an intergenerational perspective, policies may need to do even more to provide a foundation by which lower socioeconomic status individuals can attain economic mobility. Such interventions may include those directly targeted to children (e.g., early education and care provision) and those indirectly targeted to children through their parents (e.g., home visiting initiatives, income supports). 

## How Well Do Current Policies Serve Contemporary Families?

Changing demographic patterns that have brought growing diversity and complexity in U.S. family life have also resulted in key challenges to public policy with respect to parameters for eligibility and benefit determination, responsiveness to family fluidity (changes in the family unit over time), and prioritization of various family ties (biological, marital, coresident). We describe these challenges and present implications for specific programs and policies below. 

## Parameters for Eligibility and Benefit Determination

Determining who is eligible for benefits or services and how much in benefits they should receive is challenging in light of contemporary family complexity including consideration of mixed immigration status, grandparents raising grandchildren and, in some cases (e.g., vis-a-vis child support and visitation), same-sex couple families. Indeed, given that most current poli cies and programs were not designed to account for family complexity, in concert with the incremental process by which policies and programs have been modified over time and the fact that many programs are state-administered, there is no single set of criteria for assessing eligibility and benefits—in other words, defining the family “focal unit.” This is of increasing concern throughout the range of explicit and implicit policies and programs affecting families. 

SNAP, for example, uses the most inclusive approach, considering all household members that prepare and eat food together (at least half of the time) as part of the same unit. This definition allows for the inclusion in eligibility and benefit calculation of a wide range of family members such as cohabiters, step and half-siblings, and grandparents, as well as unrelated persons. Nonetheless, many children and adults today spend time living (and eating) in more than one household (e.g., children with a shared custody arrangement or adults who cohabit some of the time) and hence may not have full or continuous SNAP eligibility. (It is important to note that, despite SNAP’s inclusive approach to defining the case unit, there is widespread variation in SNAP take-up rates across states and demographic groups, including mixed-status families, which is due at least in part to variation in both eligibility and administrative burden required of applicants and recipients; see Herd & Moynihan, 2018). 

In contrast, programs administered through the tax system (EITC, CTC) use the filing status for federal income taxes as the starting point for eligibility; these are based on marital status and family situations including, in some instances, biological relationships regardless of marital or coresident status, but contingent on being at least one parent’s “dependent.” Children can only be claimed as dependents if they live more than half the year in a household, and cohabiters must file separately. The CTC excludes the child residency criterion for noncustodial parents, although only one parent can claim the child in a given year; see Maag, Peters, and Edelstein (2016) for a review and analysis of tax policies with respect to complex families and instability in family arrangements over time. The Medicaid program takes yet another approach: all children who are tax dependents (regardless of age) are included, as are any children younger than age 21 who are living in the household and are cared for by the householder, even if not a tax dependent. 

Beyond variation in federal rules, programs administered at the state and local levels may have very different eligibility and benefit crite ria. For example, within the TANF program, the extent to which benefit amounts increase by fam ily size varies considerably across states, with some states (e.g., Idaho) having a fixed payment amount (so-called “family cap”) and othe states (e.g., California) substantially increasing the benefit amount for each additional child (Falk, 2014). Also, although most states treat cohabiting and married couples similarly if the resident father is the biological father of the child(ren), if a cohabiting father is not biologically related to a child, the mother and child are treated as a single-parent family (we use gendered language here for ease of explication; Moffitt, Phelan, & Winkler, 2017). Such crite ria become particularly complicated, if not prob lematic, in cases in which a coresident parent is the biological (or married stepparent) of some but not all of the children in a household. 

Another key example includes the child support enforcement program, under which it is difficult to determine appropriate child support orders in the context of MPF. The order setting process has historically assumed that noncustodial parents (generally fathers) had all of their children with the same custodial parent (generally mother) and hence assigned a diminishing share of the noncustodial parent’s income to each additional child; yet, in the context of MPF, it is difficult to determine orders that are simultaneously “fair” from the perspective of custodial parents, noncustodial parents, and children in different households (Cancian & Meyer, 2011; Meyer, Skinner, & Davidson, 2011). All else equal (incomes, custody arrangements, number of children), children from subsequent relationships are typically afforded less child support than those from prior relationships, custodial parents are typically afforded more child support for the same number of children if those children are born to multiple noncustodial parents rather than to the same noncustodial parent, and noncustodial parents are typically ordered to pay more child support for the same number of children if those children are born to multiple partners rather than to one partner. This raises important equity, adequacy, and affordability concerns based on birth order and parentage. In addition, the child support system does not typically systematically assist noncustodial parents in achieving or increasing employment and earnings (and thus their ability to afford child support payments). Moreover, although the federal Access and Visitation Mandatory Grant Program provides funding to each of the states and territories to provide services, including the establishment of parenting time arrangements for families in the child support system, intended to increase the time children spend with their noncustodial parents, the child support system has no mechanism for enforcing visitation (Administration for Children and Families [ACF]/U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, 2019). 

Additional examples abound. Ambiguities regarding family membership, for instance, exist in health care coverage, home visiting, and family leave policies and programs. With respect to health care, even in the context of the individual mandate under the ACA, ambiguities remain as to which parent is responsible for providing coverage to children and in what circumstances. Often, such decisions are made on a case-by-case basis through custody and court proceedings and, for the most part, health coverage responsibilities are based on biological, rather than coresidential relationships; among biological parents, they may also depend on which parent’s tax filing unit includes the child(ren). Home visiting programs typically focus on the (biological) mother–child dyad and to a lesser extent (and only rarely) on coresident biological fathers. Other household and family members, including stepfathers and mothers’ cohabiting partners and nonresident biological fathers, are infrequently a primary target of these interventions and services despite the fact that these individuals may exert considerable influence on children’s caregiving environments. Family leave policies must grapple with the types of children (biological, step, resident, nonresident) for which an individual is entitled to take leave. As such, contemporary family complexity entails considerable confusion and inefficiency both for (potential) recipients and for the bureaucracies trying to serve them, and thereby contributes to the “administrative burdens” that diminish the effectiveness of government programs (Herd & Moynihan, 2018). 

## Prioritization of Various Family Ties

Beyond the structural difficulty of supporting families in a variety of different—and often changing (see later)—circumstances, high levels of family complexity also bring substantive or ethical dilemmas about which roles, relationships, or actors to privilege. Whereas in traditional nuclear families, marriage, coresidence, and biological ties are typically co-occurring, in a context of greater family complexity, many families span households, include members with differing biological ties (step parents and half-siblings), and may not be legally married. Moreover, grandparents or other relatives may play parental roles when parents cannot care for their children—either on a temporary or long-term basis—creating additional complexity and diversity in who may have responsibility for childrearing. As such, program rules must become more explicit about which aspect of family life will be emphasized; however, there is currently widespread incon sistency in such. Relatedly, family complexity implies that individuals within the same family or household may differ in their conceptual izations of who is “in” their family, and the interests and incentives of individuals within a family or household may not be identical; policies must then choose who to prioritize. 

Generally speaking, current public policy privileges biological coresident parents and provides far less support to nonresident or nonbiological parents (step and social parents) as well as other caregivers (e.g., grandparents). For example, as described by Berger (2017, p. 23), U.S. social policy “treats noncustodial parents more like non-parents than like parents,” noting that noncustodial parents’ (predominantly fathers’) primary interactions with government occur within the criminal justice system, family and criminal courts, child support enforcement system, and the Internal Revenue Service. Few government benefits, services, and supports are offered to such parents who “primarily experience government as saddling them with mandates and penalties,” despite that they may provide—and are accountable to provide—considerable time and financial support to children. In contrast, (low-income) custodial parents (predominantly mothers) are typically eligible for multiple supports and services, including the EITC, the Child Tax Credit TANF, Child Support Enforcement, SNAP, Medicaid, and (sometimes) housing assistance. To the extent that there is growing diversity in parental roles, especially for fathers, amidst high levels of family instability and complexity among low-income families (Tach et al., 2014), current policies are largely inconsistent with the realities of complex contemporary American families. Similarly, in circumstances when grandparents must become custodial parents of their grandchildren, they face a safety net that offers minimal economic security due to both eligibility rules and program implementation (Pittman, 2014, 2015). 

## Responsiveness to Family Fluidity

In addition to overall inconsistencies in the ways various family forms—and individuals therein—are treated for purposes of eligibility and benefit determination across programs, public policies are also ill equipped to provide responsive services to dynamic changes in family situations. It is well known that low-income families regularly experience considerable instability and uncertainty in employment and income (Morduch & Siwicki, 2017), as well as living arrangements and family relationships (Kalil & Ryan, 2010), within relatively short time periods. Yet, applying for benefits or changing one’s information is often a cumbersome process that may involve appearing in-person and filling out paper forms—and, in the case of noncustodial parents with a child support order, sometimes reappearing in court (there is considerable variation across states in the extent to which child support modifications can be made via administrative versus court processes). 

These processes are out of sync with the dynamic nature of contemporary families. Rather, policies and programs typically assume relative consistency in income and family composition between benefit recertification periods (most commonly at 6-month to 1-year intervals, although there is variation by program and state). Typically, benefits are not immediately or automatically adjusted in response to changes in employment or earnings—although technological developments increasingly make such automation possible. For example, although it is now technologically possible to link and electronically monitor earnings and benefits across administrative data systems, which would allow for timely and automatic benefit redeterminations as incomes change, such systems have yet to be operationalized. Similarly, families often face a time-consuming and cumbersome process of changing their benefit information in response to short-term changes in family composition and living arrangements between recertification periods despite existing technol ogy that could make such changes relatively easy to accomplish through remote electronic mechanisms (e.g., cell phone apps). 

In short, the current social welfare pol icy landscape is neither nimble nor efficient in addressing the changing needs and circumstances of recipients. At the same time, however, designing appropriate reforms is complex and variation across programs may be necessary. For example, frequent redeterminations for cash (including child support) or near-cash transfers may increase economic volatility if families lose resources as a result of small fluctuations in income or temporary changes in income or family composition at the same time that they might decrease economic volatility when they compensate for such changes in the short term or respond more quickly to longer term changes. In addition, longer periods of redetermination may be preferable in areas such as child-care subsidies if, for instance, enabling parents to retain child care during periods of unemployment encourages family and income stability and allows parents opportunities to pursue new jobs. Chapter 8 of the National Academies of Science, Engineering and Medicine (2019) report on reducing child poverty provides a broader discussion of these issues. 

## Looking Ahead: Future Directions for Family Policy Research and Policy Innovation

As we look toward the future, there are both reasons for optimism and persistent challenges to address in order for family policy to better serve the needs of contemporary American families. During the past decade, we have learned much more about the variation within and across families and the dynamic processes by which families change, providing a more complete por trait of the complexity of contemporary families. We have also learned considerably more about how individuals and their families interact with and are served by public policies and programs. This has moved the field beyond a primary focus on biological parents and full siblings to consider parents having children by more than one partner (multipartnered fertility), adults and children spending time in multiple households, the presence of extended family members (especially grandparents), same-sex parents, family members with mixed immigration status, and the role of unrelated adults (especially step or social parents) in the lives of children; also, we have seen growing awareness of the gap in family patterns and family processes by socioeconomic status, as well as of the dynamic nature of family resources and composition over time. Some of this information has come via scholars combining information from existing data sets in new ways (e.g., linking survey data with administrative data), and other information has come from new questions added to existing surveys to document increasingly complicated family arrangements, or from new studies being launched to directly explore previously unknown aspects of family life. To capture the range of family relationships, it is important for surveys to explicitly ask questions of respondents about family roles and relationships that may span households (such as asking parents about children they may have had with other partners [who live elsewhere full- or part-time], and asking children about their sibling composition, particularly half-siblings who may live in other households), as well as about full- and part-time household members, other than parents, that may play family roles (such as grandparents or romantic partners of parents; Cavanagh & Fomby, 2019). Going forward, it will also be crucial to gain a better understanding of intraand interhousehold resource sharing among the individuals in complex contemporary families. 

On the public policy side, we have also seen growing attention paid to “evidence-based policymaking” within the policy community, including government entities themselves as well as think tanks and philanthropic foundations. The Obama administration placed new emphasis on the importance of rigor and evidence for generating better results within government initiatives (Haskins & Margolis, 2015). In 2016, then House Speaker Paul Ryan (Republican, Wisconsin) and Senator Patty Murray (Democrat, Washington) sponsored the bipartisan Evidence-Based Policymaking Commission Act of 2016, which led to the appointment of the Commission on Evidence-Based Policymaking; this group of experts reviewed practices across multiple government agencies and released a major report in September 2017 that offered a host of detailed findings and recommendations about how to better emphasize privacy, rigor and transparency (Commission on Evidence-Based 

Policymaking, 2017), and provided the basis for the subsequent Foundations for Evidence-Based Policymaking Act of 2018. Within the nonprofit sector, the Pew Charitable Trusts and the John D. and Catherine T. MacArthur Foundation jointly developed and released in 2014 a guide for how evidence-based policy can promote more effective government (Pew-MacArthur, 2014); this report also incorporated insights from a number of policy scholars from both academic institutions and national think tanks. A recent ANNALS volume, edited by Haskins (2018) and titled “Evidence-Based Policy: The Movement, the Goals, the Issues, the Promise,” provides an important summary of recent developments, challenges, and opportunities for using evidence in policymaking. New evidence about families combined with a growing emphasis on the use of evidence promises to enhance family policy. In addition, some programs are increasingly recognizing the importance of taking a “family-level” approach to interventions, and such programs, which jointly consider the needs of parents and children, have shown promise (Chase-Lansdale & Brooks-Gunn, 2014), as have strategies tha emphasize coordinated services across programs to meet the multiple needs of low-income families (Sama-Miller et al., 2017). 

In short, the past decade in family policy and research thereon has been marked both by considerable progress and by ongoing struggle. As highlighted throughout this review, family policy continues to struggle with how best to serve increasingly complex contemporary fam ilies. Indeed, we view family complexity and the dynamic and changing composition of families as perhaps the most crucial issue to be considered across domains of family policy in the coming years. On the whole, however, we are optimistic that the decade to come will bring additional evidence to bear on how family pol icy can better address the needs of contemporary families.
